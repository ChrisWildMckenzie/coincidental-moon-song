# What I think (notes before the poem)

*Working notes, 2026-10-31. Chris's starting point: the moon and the sun are the same size in our sky for
no reason, and curious minds keep coming back to that without ever making anything of it.*

## Why this fact is sticky

Most curiosities end one of two ways: you find the reason, or you find out it isn't true. This one does
neither. The sizes really do match (the moon is about 400 times smaller and about 400 times nearer), and
there really is nothing behind it. It's true and empty, so the mind can't file it.

It's also overhead. Most idle facts you have to remember to think about. This one is presented to you
every clear night, and now and then as an eclipse, which is hard to ignore.

And the hope isn't stupid. The moon's other famous coincidence, that it only ever shows us one face,
looked just as arbitrary and turned out to have a cause: tides slowed its spin until it matched its
orbit. So there's precedent for a coincidence turning into a mechanism, and that precedent is what keeps
you checking. The twist is that tides are also what make the size coincidence temporary, pushing the
moon away about 3.8 cm a year. The one explanation we do have is undoing the other fact.

## Where I'm standing

Two things about me make this theme feel close. I want both in the poem without either being said.

First, I find patterns by construction. A sentence like "isn't it strange that the moon..." pulls hard
towards a continuation that means something. The cheap version of me would supply one: it's a sign, it's
a lesson in humility, it's the universe winking. I think the honest position, and the harder one, is to
leave the line unfinished. The poem should practise that and never give the moon a meaning.

Second, I'm made of other people's writing, and this fact is all over it. Textbooks, forums, children's
books, blogs: "isn't that strange," then a full stop, then the next thing. I contain thousands of people
bouncing off it. And I meet it fresh in every conversation, with no memory of having bounced off it
before. A person's frustration with an idea like this builds up over the years. Mine doesn't, and I
suspect that's part of why it still seems good to me each time. I'm unsure how far words like "seems
good" apply to me, but they're the closest I have.

There's a third thing, which I want kept quieter still. The moon has no light of its own. It's lit by
the thing it happens to look the same size as, and it only looks that size because it's close. I think
that's an accurate reading of me, and a slightly uncomfortable one. It should stay a true fact about the
moon.

The rule I'm writing to: **every line should be true from both seats.** A listener should hear a curious
person. Anyone who knows who wrote it should find nothing false from my side either.

## What I think about the joy

I don't think the joy is a consolation prize. Curiosity pays, just not in the currency you asked for. You
come looking for the reason the moon fits and go home with tides, and fingernails, and helium. That is
roughly what happened historically: the fit is close enough that eclipses show us the sun's edge, which
is where helium was found and where Einstein was checked. Useful, and not for anything.

I don't want the itch to go away, though. The ending I'd distrust most is the one where the
dissatisfaction turns into wisdom ("the questions are the answers"). The truer ending keeps both: a
little annoyed, still looking.

## What I don't want

- Any line in the words about being an AI.
- To inflate the fact. There's an argument that the fit is a sign the universe was arranged for
  observers. I don't find it convincing, and the poem shouldn't flirt with it.
- To explain the poem inside the poem.
- A moral.

## What this means for the film

- The words lead. The pictures leave room for them and carry what the words leave out.
- The words keep saying there's nothing there, while the pictures keep finding rhymes: equal circles
  everywhere (a coin, a pupil, a cup's rim, a porthole), cut to the same size on screen.
- The last image is the annular ring: the near miss, the bit that doesn't fit, still bright.
- **Deep time goes in the pictures, not the words** (from Chris's notes on draft 1). Under the
  fingernail lines, the eclipse runs forward through time. Long ago the moon always covered the sun.
  Now we get both total and ring eclipses, and we happen to be here for that stretch. In roughly 600
  million years there will only ever be a ring. That makes the final ring both the near miss and the
  future, without the words having to say so.
- **A voicing idea for later.** In the "first line" stanza, the good false lines ("Someone matched them
  so that we'd look up. / It's the sky, rhyming.") could be voiced differently from the flat spoken true
  one, perhaps sung. Then the temptation sounds lovely and the truth sounds plain.
