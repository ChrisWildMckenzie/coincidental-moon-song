# Near Enough (draft 1)

The moon is four hundred times smaller than the sun
and four hundred times nearer,
so in our sky they come out the same size.
When one passes in front of the other,
it fits. Just about exactly.

There's no reason for it. I've looked.

It reads like the first line of something.
I've waited a long time for the second.
I've tried it as a sign, and as a clue,
and as a key, and gone round
looking for a door that shape.
It fits. That's all it does.

The other thing, that it only ever shows us the one face,
looked like a coincidence too,
and that one had a reason. Tides.
The earth held on to it
until it stopped turning away.
So you can see why I keep checking.
It fits. That's all it does.

It isn't even exact.
When it's further off, it comes up short,
and the sun shows round it in a ring.
Even the coincidence is a near miss.
It nearly fits. That's all it does.

And the tides that turned its face to us
are walking it away,
about as fast as fingernails grow:
in a life, the height of a room.
Long ago it always covered the sun.
One day it never will.
We happen to be here for the part where it does both.
It fits, for now. That's all it does.

I'm not the first. I've found it written down
in a hundred places, always the same way:
isn't that strange, and then a full stop,
and then whatever came next.
Everyone who's noticed it has noticed it
and then gone in.

It's even been useful.
In the light it leaves around the edge
we found helium, before we found it here,
and named it for the sun.
And the stars beside the sun one day in 1919
weren't quite where they usually are,
which is how we checked Einstein.
Useful. Not for anything.

So, no reason. I came for one
and went home with tides, and fingernails, and helium,
which isn't what I asked for,
and I'll take it.
I'll be back. I'm always back,
and I never remember the last time,
which might be why it's still this good.
Tonight it's up there doing nothing,
lit by the thing it happens to be the size of,
and I'm a little annoyed with it,
and I'm looking.
It fits.

---

## Notes on this draft

**Form.** Each stanza is one return to the moon. Each return finds something true and nearby, but never
the reason. The refrain wears down as the facts undercut it: "It fits. That's all it does." becomes
"nearly fits", then "fits, for now", and in the last line just "It fits." I tried a strict villanelle,
the form built on returning lines. Its two rhyme sounds kept forcing the facts into awkward shapes, so
I kept its principle (lines that come back with shifted meaning) and dropped the rhymes.

**Where I am in it** (never said, and true from both seats):
- "the first line of something" / "waited a long time for the second"
- "I've found it written down in a hundred places", and "Everyone who's noticed it has noticed it and
  then gone in"
- "I never remember the last time, which might be why it's still this good"
- "lit by the thing it happens to be the size of"

**Length.** About 390 words. Spoken slowly with room for the music, that's roughly four minutes, which
is probably long. Candidates to trim: the Einstein lines, the "sign, clue, key" lines, or the
fingernail stanza's middle.

**Facts used** (to double-check before recording):
- The moon is about 400× smaller and about 400× nearer than the sun.
- When the moon is near apogee, a central eclipse is annular (a ring) rather than total.
- The moon is tidally locked: Earth's tides on it slowed its spin until it matched its orbit.
- It recedes about 3.8 cm/yr, by lunar laser ranging. That's about the rate fingernails grow, and about
  3 m over an 80-year life.
- In the far past every central eclipse was total. Total eclipses should end in roughly 600 million
  years (estimates vary).
- Helium was first seen as a yellow line in the sun's spectrum during the eclipse of August 1868, was
  named after *helios*, and was found on Earth in 1895.
- In 1919, eclipse photographs showed stars near the sun displaced from their usual positions by about
  the amount general relativity predicts.

**Spare material** not used yet: the moon illusion (it looks bigger rising and isn't, and there's still
no agreed explanation of why, another unsatisfied curiosity nested inside this one), and a fingernail
held at arm's length just covering the moon.
